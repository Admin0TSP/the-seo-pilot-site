## Content

The board approves the SEO budget. Paid is plateauing, CAC is creeping, and someone — usually a new VP of Growth — has been hired to make organic "a real channel by next year." From that moment, you have roughly twelve months to prove the thesis before patience runs out.

This is the plan we'd hand to a Series-A DTC operator on day one. It's opinionated, sequencing-aware, and built around the fact that organic compounds — meaning the work you do in month two starts paying back in month seven, not next Thursday. If you treat the first half as a marketing channel and demand revenue, you'll quit before it works. If you treat it as infrastructure and demand visible progress on leading indicators, you'll have a real channel by month nine.

### Month 0: before the work starts

Before any work ships, three things have to be true:

The technical baseline is captured — current GSC impressions, current AI citation rate across your top 20 brand-relevant queries, current Core Web Vitals on top traffic pages. Without baselines, you can't claim improvement.

The category position is articulated in one sentence. Not your brand position; your *category* position. "We are the [category] for [audience] that [differentiator]." If you can't say it, you can't get cited for it.

The internal velocity question is answered: who ships content (in-house or agency), who ships engineering changes (in-house only, no exceptions), and what's the SLA between strategy and shipped output. Most failed SEO programmes don't fail because the strategy was wrong. They fail because nothing got shipped.

### Months 1–2: foundations

The first eight weeks are unglamorous. No new content. No link-building. Just the work that makes everything after this work.

Technical audit, with fixes shipped — not a 60-page PDF that gets read once. Crawl, fix indexation issues, deduplicate near-identical pages, clean up canonical chaos on product variants, kill the JavaScript hydration delay that's tanking Core Web Vitals on mobile.

Information architecture review — does your URL structure actually map to how customers think about your category? Most Shopify stores ship with a category structure inherited from the theme, which has no relationship to how your specific buyer searches.

Topic graph mapping — list every query a real customer in your category would type or speak. Group them into clusters. This becomes the editorial roadmap for months 3–9.

Baseline measurement infrastructure — GSC, GA4 properly configured (most aren't), an AI citation tracker (Profound, Otterly, or ScrunchAI), and an attribution model that ties organic-source traffic to revenue. Get this right now or every later argument about ROI dies.

Milestone at end of month 2: Core Web Vitals green on at least the homepage and top 10 traffic pages. Topic graph documented. Baseline citation rate measured.

### Months 3–4: the first editorial pushes

Now you ship content. Start with the queries closest to commercial intent — the "[your category] for [audience]" and "[your category] vs [competitor]" pages first, the educational top-of-funnel content second.

A defensible volume for a Series-A DTC brand is 4–6 substantive editorial pages per month, plus 2–3 product-supporting pages (comparison guides, buying guides, technical specs). Less than that and the topic graph grows too slowly; more than that and quality usually collapses.

Every page ships with: entity-clear opening, `Article` schema, sourced claims, an FAQ block, and a real author byline. No anonymous content. No keyword-stuffed listicles. Each piece needs to be quotable, not just rankable.

Internal linking starts here. Every new piece links to two existing pieces; every existing piece you can reasonably edit gets a link to the new piece. The topic cluster is the unit of compounding.

Milestone at end of month 4: 16–24 new editorial pages shipped, topic graph 30% built out, first ranking improvements visible in GSC (positions moving from 20–50 into the 10–20 band).

### Months 5–6: the first revenue lift

This is where the first meaningful revenue lift typically shows up. Not a flood — a measurable shift. Organic-source revenue rises 15–35% over baseline. The early-cluster pages start ranking. First-time AI citations appear, even if irregularly.

Two things to do in this window:

Double down on whichever cluster is moving fastest. Don't spread thin. If your "for early-stage founders" cluster is converting, build three more pages in that cluster before you start the next one.

Begin link-building in earnest. By month five you have linkable assets — original research pieces, customer outcome stories, definitive guides. Reach out to category publications, podcasts, and complementary brands. The links earned in month 5–6 are what powers ranking compounding in month 9–10.

Milestone at end of month 6: 20–40% organic revenue lift vs baseline. 5–10 pages ranking in positions 1–10 for category-relevant queries. AI citation rate up 2–4x from baseline.

### Months 7–9: compounding starts

This is the inflection. The work from months 1–6 starts paying back disproportionately. Pages ranking in positions 5–10 climb to 1–3 with no new effort. New pages on already-established clusters rank faster than the original cluster pages did. AI engines that cited you once start citing you across more queries because your topic-level authority is now legible.

The risk here is complacency. The job in months 7–9 is to *expand the topic surface* — start the next two clusters now, before they're needed, so they're ready when the current ones plateau.

Also: this is when the link-building from months 5–6 starts compounding. Domain authority signals move slowly; the links earned three months ago are the ones moving rankings now.

Milestone at end of month 9: organic revenue 50–100% above month-1 baseline. 15–25 pages ranking in positions 1–10. AI citation rate 4–8x baseline. First "organic is now our highest-ROI channel" board slide.

### Months 10–12: making it durable

The last quarter is about converting a working programme into a defensible one. Two priorities:

Build the moats. Original research, proprietary data, customer-outcome stories with named brands. These are the assets competitors can't easily replicate. A category-defining piece of original research is worth more than 20 generic guides.

Capture the institutional memory. Document what worked, what didn't, the topic clusters that converted, the queries that didn't, the link partners that delivered, the link partners who ghosted. Without this documentation, the second-year programme either restarts from scratch or runs on tribal knowledge that walks out the door when someone leaves.

Milestone at end of month 12: organic is 25–40% of total acquisition. CAC on organic is 30–50% lower than paid. AI citation rate is high enough to influence branded search lift. The next year's programme is funded by the revenue this one produced.

### What kills the programme

A few patterns kill twelve-month organic plans regardless of how good the strategy is:

**Switching priorities every quarter.** "We need to pivot to TikTok" in month four kills the compounding. The work was sequenced; breaking the sequence resets the clock.

**Demanding revenue in month three.** It won't be there. If the board expects month-three revenue from organic, the channel will be killed before it works.

**Underinvesting in technical engineering.** Content + broken technical foundation = content that doesn't index. Treat engineering hours as non-negotiable.

**Ignoring the AI track entirely.** A 2026 plan that treats Google as the only retrieval engine is leaving meaningful demand on the table. The shared work (schema, entity clarity, quotable structure) helps both — but you have to know you're optimising for both.

**Outsourcing strategy without owning it.** Agencies execute. The strategy — what we are, who we're for, what queries we want to own — has to live in-house. Outsource the work; don't outsource the thinking.

### The summary

Twelve months of disciplined, sequenced work, with monthly checkpoints on the right leading indicators, gets a Series-A DTC brand to organic being a real channel. The brands that succeed treat months 1–6 as infrastructure and months 7–12 as compounding. The brands that fail try to harvest in month two and starve before the asset matures.

Build it like infrastructure. Measure it like a portfolio. And hold the line on the sequencing.

## FAQs

### What's the right total spend for this 12-month plan?

For a Series-A DTC brand doing $3M–$15M ARR, the credible range is $60K–$140K total spend across agency fees, content production, and tooling, spread across the year. Below $60K you're under-resourcing; above $140K you should be getting either heavy original research or paid engineering hours alongside the editorial work.

### Can we run this plan with a 2-person team in-house?

Two people works if both are senior — one editorial/strategy lead and one engineer with SEO instincts. The plan compresses but the sequencing holds. Below senior-level staffing, you need agency support for the first six months.

### What if we're already six months in and haven't followed this sequence?

Audit where you actually are: what's the topic graph state, what's been shipped, where are the rankings, what's the citation rate. Rebuild the plan from your current position, not from month 1. Skipping ahead is fine if the foundations are real; rewinding to redo foundations is fine if they're not.

### How do we know the plan is working at month three?

Three leading indicators: (1) GSC impressions trending up, even if clicks haven't; (2) average position improving on at least 30% of tracked queries; (3) the first AI citation appearances — even single-digit numbers — across your top 20 brand-relevant queries. If two of three aren't trending by month three, the plan needs a diagnostic, not a pivot.

### Should we hire a full-time SEO lead, or work with an agency?

Hire in-house if the role is at director level and you have engineering hours to support them. Work with an agency if you don't have either. The expensive mistake is hiring a mid-level SEO IC into a Series-A team without engineering support — they'll spend twelve months ticketing fixes that never ship.
