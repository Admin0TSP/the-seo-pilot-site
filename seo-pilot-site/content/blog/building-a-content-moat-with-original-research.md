## Content

There's a familiar arc to most content programmes. Year one, the team ships volume — 8, 10, 12 articles a month. Year two, the volume keeps going but the rankings plateau. Year three, the team realises that the competitor who ships four pieces a month but each one with original data is out-ranking them, getting cited by AI engines, and earning links they can't seem to attract. The lesson, which arrives later than it should: a content moat isn't built on volume. It's built on proprietary inputs that competitors can't easily replicate.

This is the case for original research as a content strategy, and the playbook for producing it without a 12-person research team.

### Why proprietary data compounds and volume doesn't

A generic guide on "how to choose a CRM" can be written by any team in your category in roughly the same way, with roughly the same advice. The version that ranks tends to be the one with the strongest domain authority and the largest content footprint. That's a fight you win by spending more — and someone always has more.

A piece of original research — "we analysed 412 Series-B B2B SaaS companies and found their CRM-related churn is 3.7x higher in the first 90 days post-implementation than month 4 onward" — can only be written by the team that holds the data. It can't be replicated. It can't be out-spent. And every time someone in your category writes a follow-up piece, they have to cite you.

This is the citation compounding loop that turns research into a moat:

1. You publish proprietary research with named, specific findings.
2. Other writers in your category cite the research when making claims that touch the topic.
3. Each citation feeds two signals — backlinks for Google, source authority for AI engines.
4. Your topic-level authority grows; your subsequent content gets cited more easily.
5. Competitors writing on the same topic now reference your data, which makes you the canonical source for the next round of writers.

The work involved is high. The compounding is asymmetric.

### The four research formats that actually work

Not every original research project earns links and citations. Four formats consistently do:

**Format 1: Quantitative survey of an underserved population.**

Pick a population that matters to your category, that nobody has surveyed recently, and that you can credibly reach 300+ respondents in. Examples: "What 234 in-house SEO leads at Series-A startups say they wish their agency understood." "How 412 DTC marketing directors report on organic to their boards." "The 187 most-asked questions B2B buyers run through ChatGPT before booking a demo."

The format works because surveyed data is citable, contains real numbers that quote well, and has a defensible methodology if you publish it transparently. The work: 4–8 weeks for recruitment, design, fielding, and analysis. Cost: $3K–$15K depending on whether you pay for panel access.

**Format 2: Analysis of public-but-uncollected data.**

Some of the most-cited research isn't survey data — it's analysis of public information that nobody has bothered to systematically collect. SaaS pricing pages, job listings, GitHub repos, App Store rankings, regulatory filings, public earnings call transcripts. The data exists; the analysis doesn't.

Examples: "We tracked CRM pricing pages of the top 50 B2B SaaS companies for 18 months and here's how often they changed." "We analysed 1,200 senior marketing job listings to extract the most-demanded GEO skills."

This format is faster (3–5 weeks) and cheaper (mostly analyst time) but harder to execute well — the value is in the analysis quality, not the data collection.

**Format 3: First-party data from your own product or operations.**

If you have a SaaS product, an e-commerce store, or an operating business, you have data your competitors don't. The constraint: it has to be anonymised, aggregated, and tell a story that isn't just self-promotion.

Examples: "Across 2.4M support tickets in our platform last year, here's the distribution of issue types by company size." "Across the 312 DTC brands we audited in 2025, here's the average percentage of Shopify collection pages that don't index correctly."

This is the highest-leverage format because the data is genuinely proprietary, but it has to be handled with care: anonymisation rigour, ethical sourcing, and a story that's interesting even to people who'll never become customers.

**Format 4: Longitudinal study of your category.**

Pick a metric in your category that's never been tracked over time, then track it for 12+ months, publishing every quarter. Examples: "The State of AI Citation Across the Top 100 SaaS Brands, Q1 2026." "DTC Skincare Brand Acquisition CAC by Channel — Year One."

The first edition is fine. The third edition is when this format starts compounding — at that point you have time-series data that nobody else has, and updates become the default reference work for the category.

### What kills original research projects

Five patterns sink research that should have worked:

**Bad sampling.** A "survey of marketing leaders" that pulls from your own customer list isn't representative — it's a brand-affinity report. Either source respondents independently or label the bias prominently.

**Soft methodology.** "We asked some folks in our community" is not research. Publish the n, the recruitment method, the analysis approach. If you can't defend the methodology under scrutiny, the citation rate collapses.

**Findings that say what everyone already knows.** "62% of marketers say their job is hard." Useless. Original research has to surface something non-obvious — counterintuitive, narrower than the conventional wisdom, or quantitative where everything else is qualitative.

**No quotable numbers.** A 4,000-word report with no headline-shaped data points won't get cited. The headline should be a specific number ("CAC is 3.7x higher in the first 90 days"), not a directional claim ("CAC tends to be higher early").

**Burying the data behind a form.** If you require email gating, fewer outlets will cite it (they can't link to a gated piece without their readers complaining). Publish the headline findings ungated; gate the full report if you must.

### The cost / payoff arithmetic

For a single piece of well-executed original research:

- Cost: 4–10 weeks of analyst time + $2K–$15K for survey panel or data sourcing + 2 weeks of writing/design.
- Total cost: $15K–$40K for an ambitious piece, $5K–$10K for a smaller one.

Against that:

- Links earned over 12 months: typically 40–120 dofollow backlinks for a credible piece, 200+ for a category-defining one.
- AI citations earned: typically 2–4x baseline for the parent domain on adjacent queries.
- Direct traffic and lead generation: variable, but a single piece can sometimes deliver six-figure incremental revenue across a year.
- Sales enablement value: research becomes a default deck reference, customer conversation starter, and external proof point.

Compare against the same $15K–$40K spent on, say, 6–10 generic guides. The guides each earn 0–4 backlinks, generate some organic traffic, and are functionally interchangeable with what competitors publish. Different shape of return entirely.

### How to actually execute

A practical sequence for a team running this for the first time:

**Week 1:** Pick the question. The single highest-leverage decision in the whole project. It has to be a question your audience cares about, that hasn't been answered well recently, and that you can credibly source data on.

**Weeks 2–3:** Design the data collection. Survey, public-data analysis, first-party — depending on which format. Get the methodology critiqued by someone outside your team; researchers and analysts are great here.

**Weeks 4–7:** Run the collection. Survey fielding, data scraping, analysis pipeline. Track quality metrics — response rate, completion rate, data integrity checks.

**Weeks 8–9:** Analysis and findings. The findings should fit on one slide. If they don't, you haven't analysed enough. The supporting detail can be in the report.

**Weeks 10–11:** Write and design. The format that earns the most links: a sharp executive summary up top, the findings with charts, methodology section, and the underlying data available on request or via a clean download. Avoid promotional framing entirely.

**Weeks 12+:** Distribute. Email outreach to writers in your category, syndication to relevant trade publications, social distribution, podcast pitches. The publishing is the start of the work, not the end.

### The one-line summary

You can't out-volume your competitors forever. You can out-research them once and have the asset compound for years. If your content programme has been running on volume for two years and rankings are plateauing, the next quarter shouldn't be six more guides. It should be one piece of research that only your team could have written.

## FAQs

### How often should we publish original research?

Two to four times a year is the credible cadence for most brands. Less than that and the research engine doesn't build momentum; more than that and quality usually drops. The exception: a longitudinal series can run quarterly because each edition is genuinely smaller than a standalone study.

### Can we use AI to help produce original research?

For data cleaning, statistical analysis, and chart generation — yes, with verification. For the actual primary data collection (surveys, interviews, observation) — no. The "original" in original research is the data, and AI doesn't generate that. AI is a productivity tool inside the workflow, not a substitute for the workflow.

### What if our sample size is small?

Be transparent about it. A study of 87 respondents with clean methodology is more citable than a study of 1,200 with methodological problems. Many of the most-cited pieces in B2B are n=50–150 surveys of hard-to-reach populations. The "small but rigorous" framing works when you own it.

### Do we need a PhD-level researcher to do this?

No, but you need someone who can think rigorously about sampling, bias, and methodology — typically a senior analyst or an experienced consultant. If your team doesn't have that internally, a $5K–$10K project-based engagement with a research consultant is the fastest unblock.

### Should we open-source the underlying dataset?

For maximum citation rate, yes — within whatever anonymisation constraints apply. A study with a downloadable CSV gets cited at meaningfully higher rates than a study where readers have to take your word for it. Anonymisation work upfront is the price of getting that lift.
