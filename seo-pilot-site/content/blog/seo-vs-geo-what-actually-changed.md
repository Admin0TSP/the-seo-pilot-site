## Content

Every other LinkedIn post in 2026 will tell you SEO is dead, GEO is the future, and you're behind if you haven't pivoted. None of that's quite right. SEO didn't die — it gained a sibling. The two disciplines reward overlapping work but optimise for different end results. Knowing which one matters most for *your* category is the difference between a marketing budget that compounds and one that funds the wrong thing.

### What each discipline actually optimises for

**SEO** optimises for being the highest-ranked result on a list of links. The reward is a click. Everything in the playbook — keyword research, link building, technical performance, content depth — ladders up to the goal of being in position 1–3 for queries with commercial intent.

**GEO** (Generative Engine Optimization) optimises for being the quoted source inside an AI-generated answer. The reward is a citation: your brand name and a link appear inside the answer the user reads, often above or instead of the traditional results.

The methods overlap by maybe 60%. The remaining 40% is where the disciplines diverge — and where most teams underinvest because the playbook is new.

### What each one rewards

**SEO rewards:**

- Match between query terms and on-page content
- Domain-level authority (backlinks, age, brand recognition)
- Technical performance (Core Web Vitals, mobile, crawlability)
- Internal link architecture
- Structured data that earns rich results

**GEO rewards:**

- Quotable passage density on a page
- Entity clarity in schema and prose
- Traceable, sourced claims rather than confident assertions
- Topic-level brand consistency across many pages
- Freshness signals on time-sensitive answers
- Presence in third-party retrieval indexes (Bing, Common Crawl)

The overlap is everywhere structure helps — schema markup, clean information architecture, consistent terminology. The divergence is everywhere *intent* matters — keyword-stuffed page that ranks fine in Google can be completely ignored by an AI engine because it has no quotable passages.

### Which one matters more — by category

Saying "GEO matters more in 2026" is lazy advice. The honest answer depends on what category you're in.

### B2B SaaS

GEO matters significantly more than most B2B SaaS teams realise. The reason: high-intent B2B buyers ask AI engines questions like "what's the best CRM for early-stage YC startups" before they ever click a Google result. If you're not in the cited answer, you're not in the consideration set. The conversion path increasingly runs through the chat window, not the SERP.

Priority: GEO 60%, SEO 40%.

### DTC e-commerce

Both still matter heavily. Google still drives the bulk of commercial-intent traffic — product searches, comparison queries, transactional terms — and Google Shopping is its own world. But AI engines now influence early-stage discovery for new categories ("best at-home skincare device", "kids' sunglasses that won't break"), and a brand that's cited by ChatGPT has a measurably higher add-to-cart rate from the resulting click than the same brand finding a user via paid search.

Priority: SEO 55%, GEO 45%.

### Local services

SEO still dominates. Google Maps + the local pack still drive 70%+ of the qualified leads for plumbers, dentists, interior designers, lawyers. AI engines don't have great location-aware retrieval yet, and most local searches resolve before the user ever opens a chat window. GEO is a smart hedge for the future, not the priority today.

Priority: SEO 75%, GEO 25%.

### Clinical / healthcare / regulated

GEO matters disproportionately because users increasingly research medical questions in AI engines before clicking through to authoritative sources. Citation rate is now a meaningful KPI for clinical brands — the cited source becomes the default answer, often without the user clicking anywhere.

Priority: GEO 65%, SEO 35%, with an outsized weight on sourcing rigour for both.

### The work that helps both

Roughly 60% of the playbook is shared. If you do nothing else, do this:

1. **Schema markup** — `Organization`, `Product`, `Article`, `FAQPage`. Helps Google understand entities, helps AI engines quote you accurately.
2. **Clear topic clusters** — pages on related subjects, interlinked with consistent anchor text. Helps domain authority, helps topic-level citation reputation.
3. **Quotable structure** — definitions in the first 50 words, FAQ blocks, short paragraphs. Helps featured snippets, helps citation lift-out.
4. **Sourcing discipline** — every claim has a citation. Helps EEAT for Google, helps trust scoring for AI.
5. **Freshness on time-sensitive content** — `dateModified` schema, real updates. Helps Google's "freshness" boost, helps AI engines for "best X in 2026" queries.

### The work that only helps one — and why you should still do it

**SEO-only:** technical performance, Core Web Vitals, mobile rendering, crawl budget optimisation. AI engines don't care about your LCP score. But Google does, and 65–75% of your traffic is still coming from there in 2026.

**GEO-only:** structured-data depth, AI-aware FAQ design, entity disambiguation, third-party retrieval index presence (getting properly indexed in Bing and on directories that feed Common Crawl). Google won't reward this work directly, but the compound effect of being cited by AI engines is brand-level awareness that eventually feeds back into branded search.

### The wrong question to ask

"Should I do SEO or GEO?" isn't the right framing. The right question is: *given my category, what's the right allocation of effort between the shared work and the divergent work?*

For most growth-stage brands in 2026, the answer is roughly: spend 70% of your time on the work that helps both, 20% on the GEO-only divergence, and 10% on the SEO-only divergence. That ratio shifts toward GEO for B2B and clinical, toward SEO for local and high-transactional e-commerce. But the 70% shared core stays the same no matter what.

That core is what we build with every client. The divergent 30% is where category strategy gets interesting.

## FAQs

### Is SEO actually dying?

No. Google still drives 65–80% of qualified organic traffic for most brands in 2026, depending on category. SEO is being augmented by GEO, not replaced. The teams claiming SEO is dead usually mean keyword-stuffed thin content is dead — which is true and has been true for years.

### Can I do GEO without an agency?

Yes, the basics are accessible. Schema markup, FAQ structure, and sourcing discipline are all things a marketing team can implement in-house. Where agencies add value is in the strategic prioritisation (which queries matter for your category), citation-rate tracking, and the AI-aware content production that takes practice to do well.

### How do I measure GEO success?

Three leading indicators: (1) citation rate — appearance frequency in ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Claude across your top 20 brand-relevant queries; (2) AI-attributed referral traffic in your analytics; (3) branded search lift, which compounds as AI citations build brand awareness. Tools like Profound, Otterly, and ScrunchAI track citation rate at scale.

### Will AI engines eventually replace Google?

Unlikely fully. They'll continue eating share for informational and comparison queries while Google holds onto transactional, navigational, and high-trust queries. By 2028 the smart money is on Google holding 55–65% of organic traffic and AI engines holding 25–35%, with the remainder fragmented across vertical platforms.

### What's the single biggest GEO mistake brands make?

Treating it as "SEO with extra steps." GEO rewards structurally different content — passage-level quotability, sourcing density, entity clarity — that traditional SEO playbooks don't optimise for. Brands that copy-paste their SEO checklist and add a schema block underperform brands that rebuild their content with the citation graph in mind from the start.
