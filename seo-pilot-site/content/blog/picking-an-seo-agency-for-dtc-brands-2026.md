## Content

If you're hiring an SEO agency for a DTC brand in 2026, you're probably making this decision under pressure. CAC is up. Paid is plateauing. The board wants organic to be a "real channel" by next quarter. And the agency landscape is — let's be honest — a mess of nearly-identical websites making the same claims.

This guide is the framework we wish more buyers used when they shop us against alternatives. It's also a quick way to figure out whether a current agency is actually working out, or whether the monthly retainer is buying slide decks rather than revenue.

### The five things that actually matter

### 1. Revenue attribution, not ranking reports

The single biggest difference between an agency you should hire and one you shouldn't is whether they speak in revenue or in rankings.

Ranking reports are easy. They tell you Position 8 became Position 4. They don't tell you whether that movement drove a single dollar of revenue. A serious agency builds the attribution chain from the start: which keywords are commercial-intent, which pages convert, what the assisted-conversion lift looks like, and how organic compares to paid CAC on a like-for-like basis.

The question to ask in the sales call: *"At month six, what numbers will you show me on a board slide that aren't traffic or rankings?"*

If they pause too long, that's your answer.

### 2. Technical depth on your specific stack

DTC SEO in 2026 is heavily technical. You're probably on Shopify, BigCommerce, or a headless setup with Next.js / Remix on top. Each has its own SEO traps: collection-page canonical issues, product variant indexing chaos, faceted-navigation overload, JavaScript hydration timing.

A good agency will, before they pitch, run a crawl on your site and bring 3–5 specific technical issues to the first meeting. Not generic "your site speed could be better" — actually specific, like "your collection pages are setting canonicals to themselves but your variant pages are competing for the same intent, here's the fix."

If their pitch deck is the same one they'd show a B2B SaaS company, walk.

### 3. Content velocity that matches your category

Different categories need different volume. A boutique luxury brand might need 1–2 long-form articles a month, each painstakingly produced. A category-defining new product (think: novel skincare device, new beverage category) might need 8–12 educational pieces a month to build the topic graph from scratch.

Ask: *what's a reasonable content velocity for our category, and why?* If the answer is "we'll do 4 articles a month" without any reference to your specific category dynamics, they're running a templated model. That's fine for some brands; bad for others.

### 4. An AI citation strategy that isn't an afterthought

In 2026, "SEO that doesn't think about AI engines" is "SEO that's 18 months behind." Ask specifically:

- How do they track AI citation rate?
- What changes do they make to content to improve citability?
- Do they have a process for the divergence between SEO-only work and GEO-only work?

If the answer is "we'll add some FAQ schema and call it GEO," they're rebranding 2023 SEO. If they can talk through entity clarity, passage quotability, third-party retrieval indexes, and the trade-offs between optimising for Google vs. AI engines — they've actually been doing the work.

### 5. Transparency you can verify, not transparency they describe

"Transparent reporting" is the most over-used phrase in agency sales decks. What does it actually mean in practice?

- You have **direct access** to GSC, GA4, and any tool they use (Ahrefs, Semrush, Screaming Frog). Not their consolidated dashboard — the source systems.
- The monthly report includes the **work that didn't work**, not just the wins.
- The deliverables are **itemised**, with named pages/links/audits — not "10 hours of strategy."
- The Slack or email response time is **stated up-front** and held to.

If you can't get a straight answer on any of those four, you'll be paying for opacity for the next year.

### The three red flags

### Red flag 1: Slide-deck-only deliverables

Some agencies bill for "strategy" without ever shipping a thing. You should receive code changes, content drafts, link placements, and audit fixes — actual artefacts. If month four arrives and you've received slides but no shipped work, your retainer is paying for someone's PowerPoint subscription.

### Red flag 2: No GSC access on day one

If an agency wants to "set up tracking" for two weeks before they look at your Search Console data, they're stalling. Real SEO work starts the day you hand over GSC + GA4 access. The first audit should come back within 5–7 working days.

### Red flag 3: Fixed-package "SEO audits"

"$2,000 for a full SEO audit." This is almost always a templated report — auto-generated from Ahrefs or Semrush, lightly edited, presented in an hour-long meeting. Real audits cost more, take longer, and are specific to your site's architecture and category. The fixed-package version is fine as a starting point but should never be the basis for a long-term retainer.

### What real transparency looks like

Here's what we deliver every month to every client, and what you should expect from anyone:

- **A 1-page summary** with three numbers: organic-attributed revenue, organic-driven leads, and AI-citation rate. No charts that need explaining.
- **An itemised deliverables list** showing every page edited, every link built, every audit fix shipped. Dated and linkable.
- **A "what didn't work" section** with at least one experiment we ran that flat-out failed, and what we learned.
- **A 60-minute monthly call** where you bring questions and we don't show slides.
- **A shared Notion or Linear board** with every in-flight item, updated daily.

That's the bar. It's not exotic. But it's much rarer in this industry than it should be.

### When in-house beats agency

Not every brand needs an agency. If you have:

- A senior SEO/content lead on staff already
- An engineer who can ship technical SEO changes without ticketing
- A content team producing 6+ articles a month
- And the discipline to do consistent measurement

...you'll usually outperform an agency at lower cost. The maths flips when any of those four are missing. Agencies earn their keep by being the team you don't have to recruit, manage, or retain.

### How we'd hire ourselves

If you were the marketing lead at a Series-A DTC brand hiring TheSEOPilot tomorrow, here's the test we'd want you to run on us:

1. Send three site URLs from your competitive set. Ask for an unfiltered teardown of all four (yours plus three competitors), specifically calling out where we'd start work first and why.
2. Ask for two case studies in roughly your category, with the actual numbers.
3. Ask for the name of the human on our team who'd lead your account.
4. Ask what we'd refuse to do for you.

Number 4 is the most diagnostic. Any agency that says "we can do anything" is selling you a fiction. The good ones know what they're not.

### The one-line summary

Hire the agency whose work would still be useful even if their monthly retainer ended next month. If everything they did is locked inside their tools, their dashboards, or their team's heads, you're renting visibility instead of building it.

## FAQs

### What's a fair monthly retainer for a Series-A DTC brand?

In 2026, $4,000–$9,000/month is the credible range for a brand doing $1M–$10M in revenue. Below $4K you're usually getting templated work or junior staff. Above $9K you should be getting either heavy content production (10+ articles/month) or significant technical engineering hours. Outside that range, ask hard questions about what you're paying for.

### How long until I see meaningful results?

For a DTC brand starting from zero or near-zero organic traffic, expect: month 1–2, foundations and technical fixes; month 3–4, first ranking improvements; month 5–6, first measurable revenue lift; month 9–12, the compounding kicks in. Anyone promising "results in 30 days" is either lying or planning to over-index on quick-win keywords that won't sustain.

### Should I hire a generalist agency or a DTC specialist?

For brands above $5M ARR, a DTC specialist is worth the premium because they know the platform-specific traps. Below $5M, a strong generalist who'll learn your stack is often a better economic choice — provided they pass the "three competitors teardown" test.

### Is it OK to switch agencies if it's not working?

Yes, and most brands wait too long to. The signal isn't "we haven't ranked yet" — that's normal at month four. The signal is "I can't get a straight answer about what you're actually doing." If communication breaks down before results land, results won't land.

### Can I trial an agency before committing to a long retainer?

Yes — a one-off audit + 60-day pilot project is a reasonable structure, with both sides agreeing to convert to retainer if the pilot lands. Any agency that refuses a structured pilot probably hasn't built one that consistently de-risks the buying decision. That's a yellow flag on its own.
