## Content

A page can be well-researched, well-written, and well-ranked in Google — and still never get cited by an AI engine. The difference between content that gets cited and content that doesn't is rarely depth or quality. It's structure. AI engines don't lift arguments, they lift passages. A page that holds an excellent argument across 1,200 words but contains no individually quotable sentence will lose every citation race to a less sophisticated page where every paragraph could stand alone.

This is a working writer's guide for producing content that AI engines actually quote. It's opinionated. It will sometimes conflict with how you've been trained to write. That conflict is the point — most "good" writing is bad for citation.

### What "quotable" actually means

A model deciding whether to cite a passage is making three implicit judgments in milliseconds:

1. Does this sentence stand alone? If lifted out of its paragraph, does it still make sense?
2. Does it answer the query directly, or does it require the reader to also pull surrounding context?
3. Is the source traceable enough that the model can defensibly attribute the claim?

A passage that scores well on all three gets cited. A passage that requires three paragraphs of context to make sense will be ignored even if it's the best content on the page. The structural work is making sure your sentences pass all three tests.

### The eight structural moves

#### Move 1: Lead with the definition

Every page that defines a concept, product, or category should put the definition in the first 30–50 words. Not as a teaser. Not after a clever opening anecdote. The actual definition, in the actual first sentence.

The pattern that works: `[Subject]` is `[direct definition]` that `[key differentiator]`. So instead of:

> *Have you ever wondered what makes some skincare devices more effective than others? In today's market, brands are racing to incorporate new technologies that promise faster results...*

write:

> A microvibration skincare device is a handheld electrical device that uses high-frequency oscillation (typically 8–12 kHz) to enhance product absorption and stimulate microcirculation in the skin.

The first version is a magazine intro. The second is a citable lead. AI engines will quote the second; they won't quote the first.

#### Move 2: One claim per sentence

Long sentences with multiple clauses bury the citable claim inside structural complexity. The model can't reliably extract a sentence with three nested conditionals. It can lift a sentence that makes one clear claim.

Before:
> While many DTC brands have struggled with conversion rates in recent years, particularly those operating in saturated categories like skincare and supplements, the brands that have successfully adapted typically show 8–12% conversion improvements after restructuring their product pages.

After:
> DTC brands in saturated categories like skincare and supplements typically see 8–12% conversion improvements after restructuring their product pages. The brands that don't restructure plateau.

Same information, two clean sentences. The second one is the citable claim.

#### Move 3: Numbers in the same sentence as the claim

Models reliably cite passages with quantified claims. A claim without a number is decorative. A claim with a number is evidence.

Replace:
> *Conversion rates tend to improve significantly when social proof is added above the fold.*

With:
> Adding customer photos above the fold on a product page typically lifts conversion rate by 8–12%.

The numeric version is citable. The vague version is not. If you don't have a number for a claim, either find one, qualify the claim more precisely, or remove it.

#### Move 4: Name your sources inline

A passage with an attributable source becomes citable in a way an unattributed claim does not. The convention that works: name the source in the same sentence as the claim, not in a footnote or at the end of the paragraph.

Replace:
> *Recent studies have shown that AI engines now drive a meaningful share of pre-purchase research.*

With:
> A 2026 Pew Research survey found that 38% of Gen Z buyers consult ChatGPT or Perplexity before making purchase decisions in beauty and skincare.

The inline attribution does several jobs at once: gives the model a source to cite alongside your claim, distinguishes your content from unsourced AI-generated content, and reinforces your domain's trust profile across the engine's citation graph.

#### Move 5: Use FAQ blocks for the actual questions

Every page that touches a topic should have an explicit FAQ block answering the questions people actually ask. Not because of `FAQPage` schema as a checkbox — because the FAQ block is the highest-citation-rate unit on a typical page. Models lift Q/A pairs more cleanly than they lift narrative prose.

The FAQ format that works: 4–8 specific questions, written the way people actually type them (lowercase, conversational), each with a 2–4 sentence answer. The first sentence of each answer should fully answer the question; the next 1–3 sentences add precision.

Don't write the questions you wish people asked. Write the questions they actually ask. You can find these by running your category through People Also Ask, AnswerThePublic, or by examining the question-shaped queries in your own GSC data.

#### Move 6: Define every term you use

Technical content fails to get cited because of terminological ambiguity. A page that uses "CRO", "CRO" (Chief Revenue Officer), and "Conversion Rate Optimization" interchangeably across different sections is invisible to a model trying to determine what the page is *about*.

The pattern: introduce each term explicitly the first time you use it. "Conversion rate optimization (CRO) is the practice of..." — then you can use the abbreviation freely afterwards. This applies to all jargon: industry acronyms, product names, framework names, technical concepts.

#### Move 7: Use the canonical names

Models build entity graphs based on canonical names. A page that calls Microsoft Excel "Excel", "MS Excel", and "spreadsheet software" inconsistently fragments its entity association. A page that consistently uses "Microsoft Excel" gets pulled cleanly into the engine's entity for that tool.

The same applies to category names. If your category has a canonical name, use it consistently. If there's no canonical name yet, pick one and use it consistently — that consistent usage is part of how you become the source for the term in the engine's training data.

#### Move 8: Headers that pose answerable questions

The most cited headers are the ones that pose the question the reader is actually asking. "How AI Engines Decide What to Cite" is citable. "Understanding the New Search Landscape" is not — it's not specific enough to answer any particular query.

Pattern: write your headers as the search query a user might type. "What is X?", "Why does Y happen?", "How do you fix Z?" — these patterns map directly to AI engine retrieval and signal exactly what each section answers.

### Three rewriting patterns for existing content

Most teams have months of existing content that's failing the citability tests above. The good news: this is fixable in editing passes, not in expensive new content production. Three high-leverage rewriting patterns:

**Pattern 1: The opening rewrite.** Take any page where the first 100 words are anecdotal, narrative, or scene-setting. Replace with a direct definition or claim. Keep the original opening as the second paragraph if it adds context. Three hours of work, often doubles a page's citation rate.

**Pattern 2: The compound-sentence break.** Scan any existing page for sentences longer than 25 words. Most of them can be split into two cleaner sentences without losing meaning. This single edit improves both citation rate and human readability simultaneously.

**Pattern 3: The FAQ addition.** Many pages have an "FAQ-shaped" content opportunity buried in the middle — questions implicit in the body that could be surfaced as an explicit FAQ block. Pulling them out and structuring them as Q/A pairs adds a new citable unit to the page without writing new content.

### A 15-minute citability audit for any page

Open a page you've written. Run through this checklist:

1. **Open the page.** Is the first sentence a definition or claim? Or is it scene-setting? If scene-setting, mark for rewrite.

2. **Find your longest sentence.** Count the words. If over 25, mark for splitting.

3. **Look for numbers.** Does the page have specific, sourced numbers in the body? Or vague qualifiers like "significant", "substantial", "many"? Vague claims aren't citable.

4. **Find an unattributed claim.** Pick any factual claim on the page. Could a reader trace it to a source from what's written? If not, the claim needs an inline attribution or it needs to be removed.

5. **Check for an FAQ block.** Does the page have an explicit FAQ section answering 4+ specific questions? If not, this is the single highest-yield addition you can make.

6. **Read your headers.** Do they answer questions a reader might search, or are they decorative? Headers that don't map to search queries don't help citation.

7. **Audit terminology consistency.** Search the page for any term used 3+ times — is it spelled and used identically each time? Inconsistency fragments entity matching.

Running this on the top 10 pages of a site and applying the resulting fixes typically lifts citation rate within 6 weeks. The work is editing, not writing. The leverage is unusual.

### Why this writing is good for humans too

A useful note: every move described here also improves the page for human readers. Definitions up front help readers orient. One-claim-per-sentence improves comprehension. Inline attribution builds trust. FAQ blocks are scannable. Consistent terminology reduces cognitive load.

This is not coincidental. AI engines are trained to recognise the structural features that human readers find clearest. Writing for citation isn't writing for machines; it's writing more clearly. The pages that get cited are the same pages that get read, shared, and trusted.

### The one-line summary

A page is citable when every sentence could stand alone, every claim has a number or a source, and the structure answers questions a real person would type. The best writing for AI engines is just disciplined writing — without the magazine-intro habit, without the comma-stacked clauses, and without the vague qualifiers. Make every sentence pull its weight, and the citations follow.

## FAQs

### How long are these editing-pass improvements visible in citation rate?

Four to six weeks after the changes go live, in our experience across roughly 60 client edits. AI engines re-crawl high-traffic pages within 1–2 weeks but the engine's retrieval scoring needs an additional 3–4 weeks of cycles to update.

### Will writing this way hurt our brand voice?

No, but it shifts what "voice" means. The work above doesn't constrain word choice, opinion strength, or stylistic flair. It constrains sentence structure. A brand can have a strong, opinionated, distinctive voice while still leading with definitions, keeping sentences clean, and including FAQ blocks. The brands losing voice are the ones who never had a strong voice — they just had a tone of corporate vagueness.

### What if our category has no canonical name?

You have a strategic opportunity. Pick one name, use it consistently across all your content, and you become a meaningful contributor to how the model learns the term. If the category eventually settles on a different name, you can rewrite — but in the meantime, you've established yourself as a source. The brands that named "category creation" categories early often end up as the canonical reference even after the language evolves.

### Should we use AI to help write this kind of content?

For first drafts, yes — modern models produce competent first drafts that follow most of these patterns when prompted well. The work that AI doesn't do well: the inline attribution to real sources, the specific numbers from your own data, and the editorial judgement about which claims are worth making. Use AI for productivity, human writers for substance.

### How does this writing approach affect Google rankings?

Positively, in nearly every case. The structural moves above improve readability metrics (time on page, scroll depth), featured snippet probability, and rich-result eligibility. Google has been moving toward this exact kind of structured, clear writing for years. The bonus is that AI engines reward the same patterns more aggressively, so the same editing work pays back across both retrieval systems.
